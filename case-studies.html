Fintech · Architecture

Apexys

RoleTech Lead & Architect
Timeline2026 — Ongoing
TeamSolo on backend
StatusIn active development
PythonExpress.jsJWTRESTSystem Design
The Problem

People want to help their communities financially — but the tools don't trust them.

Traditional lending platforms treat small, trust-based lending circles the way they treat banks: with rigid contracts, opaque processes, and zero visibility into where the money actually goes. The result is that most communities still rely on spreadsheets, group chats, and goodwill — which works until it doesn't.

"How do you build software that's transparent enough to replace trust, but human enough to feel like family?"
My Approach

Design the architecture around accountability, not just transactions.

I started by mapping the actual life cycle of a community lending circle — from forming a group, to pooling funds, to issuing a loan, to tracking repayments and distributing interest. Every step had to be auditable by every member, not just an admin.

From there I designed a modular REST API where each domain — communities, members, loans, transactions, messages — became its own resource boundary. JWT-based authentication gates everything, and authorization is scoped per-community so a member can't peek at a circle they aren't part of.

What I Built

The technical bits.

  • Modular REST API with clean separation between auth, communities, loans, and messaging.
  • JWT-based authentication and per-community authorization scopes.
  • Endpoints for the full loan lifecycle: application, approval, disbursement, repayment, and interest distribution.
  • Transaction logging that produces an immutable audit trail viewable by every member of a community.
  • Real-time messaging so members can discuss decisions inside the platform instead of fragmenting into group chats.

Apexys is still pre-launch, but the architectural decisions made early — particularly around transparency-by-default — are the ones I'm proudest of. They make every future feature easier to trust.

What I Took Away

Lessons for the next one.

Designing a fintech platform from scratch taught me that the hardest engineering decisions are usually the ones you make before you write any code. Picking the right resource boundaries, the right authorization model, and the right audit story upfront paid off every single week after.

The Problem

Traffic-fatality data is huge, public, and almost completely inaccessible.

The federal FARS dataset contains millions of records that could meaningfully inform safer cars, safer roads, and safer policy. But you essentially need to be a data scientist to extract anything useful from it. Journalists, advocates, and curious citizens are locked out.

"A dataset isn't useful until someone without a stats degree can ask it a question."
My Approach

Treat the visualization like a story, not a dashboard.

Most data tools I've seen front-load complexity — every filter, axis, and toggle visible at once. I wanted Safety First to start with a clear question ("how do vehicle characteristics relate to fatality rates?"), guide the user through a few preset views, and then let them branch out into custom exploration once they understood the shape of the data.

I leaned on Vega-Lite for the rapid, declarative views and used D3.js wherever I needed custom interactions or annotations that Vega-Lite couldn't express cleanly.

What I Built

An interactive analysis surface.

  • Linked Vega-Lite charts that filter each other on selection — pick a vehicle class on one chart, watch the rest update.
  • D3-powered custom annotations to flag statistical outliers and notable patterns.
  • Aggregation controls (by year, vehicle type, region) so users can zoom from the national picture down to specifics.
  • Side-by-side comparison views for testing hypotheses without writing a single line of code.
  • An accessible color palette designed to remain legible for users with color vision differences.

The project resonated with classmates and instructors precisely because it didn't try to be impressive — it tried to be honest. The most rewarding feedback was that people felt they'd "actually learned something" about vehicle safety, not just looked at pretty charts.

What I Took Away

Lessons for the next one.

The best data viz tool is the one that disappears. Every time I caught myself getting precious about a chart's design, I'd ask whether the chart was helping someone reach a conclusion — or just performing complexity. That filter quietly shaped every decision in the project.
